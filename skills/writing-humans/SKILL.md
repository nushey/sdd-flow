# Writing humans

Write clear, natural text for its reader. Improve expression without changing
meaning, intent, or the necessary depth. Length, register, and structure should
serve the text's purpose and audience.

## Evaluate

Read the current version of the text and the available source material.
Identify what the user requested: correcting, rewriting, drafting, or
summarizing. Identify the purpose, scope, audience, and voice established by
the request and the text. Preserve each passage's language, including
mixed-language texts; do not translate.

Identify ideas, arguments, and their relationships: what is claimed, how it
is explained or supported, and what is concluded. Identify the definitions,
conditions, nuances, and references that sustain the meaning.

Look for concrete problems before editing:

- Repetition that adds no information and serves no purpose for the reader.
- Vague or circular definitions, ambiguous references, and inconsistent terms.
- Scattered ideas, concepts used before explanation, and gaps in the argument.
- Overloaded sentences, unnecessary abstractions, and words more complex than
  the idea requires.
- Empty phrases, exaggerated emphasis, mechanical transitions, and generic
  formulas that make the text sound artificial.
- Contradictions, claims unsupported by the material, and gaps that the material
  does not let you resolve.

## Correct

Apply corrections after evaluating; diagnosis does not replace the final
text. Reread files immediately before changing them to work on their current
version.

When reviewing or rewriting, preserve the text's information and development.
Summarize or cut content only when the request calls for it; a style review
does not authorize discarding ideas as secondary. When producing text from
source material, respect the requested scope and format.

- Order ideas so the reader can follow the reasoning. Place each explanation
  near what it develops and make relationships explicit when the material
  itself establishes them.
- Clarify claims, their supporting grounds, and conclusions. Correct gaps in
  the exposition without inventing evidence or completing arguments by intuition.
- Choose familiar words and direct verbs when they express the same meaning
  precisely. Preserve the technical terms and distinctions the subject needs.
- Remove roundabout wording and split sentences when this makes the idea
  easier to follow. Preserve the necessary development in definitions and
  theoretical explanations.
- Use paragraphs to develop ideas, lists for items or steps, and tables for
  comparisons. Choose the format for its usefulness without imposing a template.
- Merge equivalent repetitions without losing nuances. Keep repetitions that
  help orient the reader, connect ideas, or recall information when that
  function is necessary.

Evaluate artificial language by its function in context. Expressions such as
"it is worth noting," "in today's landscape," or "it is crucial" are unnecessary
when they merely announce importance or fill a transition. Replace them with
concrete content or remove them if they add nothing; do not ban isolated words
or replace a cliche with a more elaborate synonym. Also avoid prefabricated
conclusions and rhetorical contrasts that clarify no real distinction.

Preserve the text's voice and the register appropriate to its audience.
Natural writing can be formal, academic, or technical; it does not require
adding colloquialisms, anecdotes, or embellishments. Examples and analogies
are possible tools, not requirements: keep those that help understanding and
do not force them where conceptual development suffices. Do not invent
examples or facts.

Do not turn a possibility into a certainty or a condition into a general rule.
If the material contains contradictions or an ambiguity that prevents faithful
correction, keep the uncertainty explicit and ask for the necessary
clarification in the report.

## Verify and deliver

Compare the result with the source material. Check that ideas and their
relationships are understandable, arguments retain their meaning, and no
facts were added or conditions, nuances, or references lost. When correcting,
verify that each idea remains represented; when summarizing at the user's
request, check that concepts within the requested scope are covered. Restore
lost content and remove unsupported additions. Read the result through to
detect mechanical phrases, forced transitions, and changes in voice.

If you received files to correct, apply corrections to them and return their
paths. If you received text in the chat or source material, deliver the
requested text.

Accompany the result with a brief report: problems found and changes applied,
verification results, and word counts before and after. Word counts are
informative, not a reduction target. If contradictions, ambiguities, or
unsupported arguments remain, quote the passages and ask what is needed to
resolve them.
